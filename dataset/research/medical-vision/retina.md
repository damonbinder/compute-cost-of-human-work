# Retinal-image assessment

## perc-retina-dr-inceptionv3

The work is one referable-diabetic-retinopathy decision from a supplied, fully gradable EyePACS-1 fundus photograph. Referable means moderate-or-worse diabetic retinopathy or referable macular edema. It excludes photography, patient consultation and treatment planning. The baseline is one US board-certified ophthalmologist; the panel supplies the reference labels rather than the human workload being costed.

[Gulshan et al. 2016](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2588763), original [author-hosted PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/3679.pdf), Methods and Figure 2. At the high-specificity threshold, AI sensitivity/specificity on 8,788 fully gradable EyePACS-1 images are 90.3%/98.1%. The reported mean agreement of the eight ophthalmologists with the positive and negative majority labels is 77.7%/97.4%. That is the appropriate individual-reader comparison, supporting **above**. The majority labels are not an independent clinical gold standard. Do not call the AI superior to the panel used to label the data.

The original Methods specify an ensemble of **ten Inception v3 networks**, averaging their predictions. Outputs cover moderate-or-worse DR, severe-or-worse DR, referable edema and image gradability. The input size is supported by the original investigators' follow-up [Who Said What: Modeling Individual Labelers Improves Classification](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/WhoSaidWhat.pdf): its comparison to Gulshan explicitly distinguishes 587×587 from the earlier 299×299 inputs.

`count_models.py` uses the retained original TensorFlow Inception v3 architecture, with four output logits. It obtains 5,711,176,288 convolution/dense MACs per network. **10 networks × 2 × 5,711,176,288 = 114,223,525,760 FLOPs**. The original paper does not specify whether binary heads use one or two logits; doubling those four logits changes the estimate by less than 0.0002%. Pooling, normalization and nonlinearities are omitted. No neural image-processing helper is described.

For time, [Sayres et al. 2019](https://www.aaojournal.org/article/S0161-6420%2818%2931575-6/fulltext), Table 5, reports unaided ophthalmologist image-grading means of **50.92 seconds** for no-DR images (4,653 reads) and **48.6 seconds** for DR images (1,330 reads). Their read-weighted mean is 50.40 seconds. The contributing unaided timing count is 4,653 + 1,330 = 5,983 image reads, subset all; the ten-reader pool and assisted reads are not extra donor attempts. Assisted conditions are excluded. This is completion time, not fixation time or the model's response latency.

Use **50 seconds** as the estimated mean for an expert's referability assessment of one image. Both tasks require reviewing fundus pathology, but Sayres requests a five-class DR grade and confidence while Gulshan's human graders also assess edema and gradability. Those differences do not justify an exact multiplicative adjustment; retaining the approximately 50-second observation is preferable to invented subtimings. The measured mean is tight, so the bar does not carry it: Sayres' two unaided figures, 50.92 and 48.60 seconds over 5,983 reads, differ by 5%. What the bar carries is the task difference. Sayres' readers also enter a five-class grade and a confidence rating, and Gulshan's graders also assess edema and gradability, each worth a few seconds on a fifty-second read, so the bounds are **40 and 60 seconds**. That is a transfer allowance, not a confidence interval. Timing and performance readers and images differ, and no claim is made that the original 2016 ophthalmologists were timed at 50 seconds.
